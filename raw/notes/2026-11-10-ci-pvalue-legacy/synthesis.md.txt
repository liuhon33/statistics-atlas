# Regression inference through projection geometry

## What the axes and projections mean

For $n$ observations in simple regression, place the response vector $y=(y_1,\ldots,y_n)^\top$ in $\mathbb R^n$. Each coordinate is an observation's response. The predictor vector $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)^\top$ and all-ones vector $\mathbf1$ span the allowed mean space
$$\mathcal M=\{\beta_0\mathbf1+\beta_1x:\beta_0,\beta_1\in\mathbb R\}.$$
If $x$ is nonconstant, $\dim\mathcal M=2$. With three observations it is a plane in $\mathbb R^3$. The coefficients label combinations of its spanning vectors; they are not the three response axes.

Write $P_{\mathcal M}$ for orthogonal projection onto the plane and $P_{\mathbf1}$ for projection onto the intercept line. Then
$$\widehat\mu=P_{\mathcal M}y,\qquad e=y-\widehat\mu.$$
The no-slope fit is $P_{\mathbf1}y=\bar y\mathbf1$, where $\bar y=n^{-1}\sum_i y_i$. The note's $P_{\mathcal M}^{\perp}$ means the **complementary projector** $I-P_{\mathcal M}$, not an inverse. Source: pp. 1–2.

Centering the predictor makes the picture easier to read. With $x_c=x-\bar x\mathbf1$ and $S_{xx}=\|x_c\|^2>0$, an orthonormal basis for the plane is
$$e_1=\frac{\mathbf1}{\sqrt n},\qquad e_2=\frac{x_c}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}.$$
Thus $\widehat\mu=(y^\top e_1)e_1+(y^\top e_2)e_2$. The first component gives the mean response, and the second gives the fitted trend. These are the normalized directions in the user's p. 2 calculation.

## From a longer residual to an F test

The intercept-only residual contains the full-model residual plus the fitted trend component. The two components are orthogonal. Their squared lengths therefore add, so the reduction in RSS equals the squared distance between the two fitted points.

[[wiki/equations/Nested regression F test|The canonical F-test page]] supplies the full formula and proof. It divides the reduction by the number of added directions, then compares it with the full residual variance estimate. Under the null and Gaussian spherical noise, the numerator and denominator come from independent chi-square components.

For the three-point drawing, the full plane leaves **one** residual direction, while the intercept-only line leaves **two**. The added direction contributes one degree of freedom. A large F ratio means the observed improvement is unusually large under the no-trend model, relative to the residual noise estimate. It is not by itself a posterior probability for either model or proof of a practically important effect.

## Your hand-drawn angle and rejection-region picture

![[raw/assets/2026-11-02-my-handdrawn-regression-geometry.png|850]]

Your original image is preserved unchanged. It is a schematic of fit/residual directions and rejection regions, with the visible ratio $\cot\theta=b/a$. The accompanying [[wiki/sources/Regression slope derivations and geometry - 2026-11-02|summary §§4–6]] supplies the precise interpretation below; unspecified axes or colored surfaces are not assigned extra quantitative meaning.

Start from the [[wiki/equations/OLS coefficient covariance and standard errors#Slope as correlation times scale|checked slope identity]]. For nonconstant centered $x_c,y_c$, let $r=r_{xy}$ and $\theta=\angle(x_c,y_c)$. Write $\widehat y_c=\widehat\beta_1x_c$ and $e=y_c-\widehat y_c$. Orthogonality gives
$$\operatorname{RSS}=\|e\|^2=(1-r^2)\|y_c\|^2,\qquad R^2=r^2.$$
Here $R^2$ is the ordinary centered goodness-of-fit measure for simple regression with an intercept. It is not a general claim that multiple-regression $R^2$ is any one predictor's squared marginal correlation.

For $n>2$ and positive RSS, the usual estimated standard error and zero-slope statistic become
$$\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\widehat\beta_1)
=\frac{s_y}{s_x}\sqrt{\frac{1-r^2}{n-2}},\qquad
t=\frac{\widehat\beta_1}{\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\widehat\beta_1)}
=r\sqrt{\frac{n-2}{1-r^2}}
=\sqrt{n-2}\cot\theta.$$
The sign is retained because $\theta\in(0,\pi)$ can be obtuse. In the sketch's **ratio of lengths**, interpret $b$ as $L_{\rm fit}=\|\widehat y_c\|$ and $a$ as $L_{\rm res}=\|e\|$; these are side lengths, not the candidate slope $b$ used elsewhere. Then
$$\frac{L_{\rm fit}}{L_{\rm res}}=|\cot\theta|,\qquad
F=t^2=(n-2)\frac{L_{\rm fit}^2}{L_{\rm res}^2}.$$
The denominator is remaining **residual length**, rather than a separate measure of fitted length. If the fitted vector is nonzero, its acute angle with $y_c$ gives this unsigned ratio. If the fitted vector is zero, the length ratio is zero even though an angle involving that zero vector is undefined.

Under the [[wiki/equations/Classical regression t inference|classical Gaussian assumptions]] and $H_0:\beta_1=0$, a two-sided level-$\alpha$ test rejects when
$$|\cot\theta|>\frac{t_{n-2,1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{n-2}}.$$
Alignment close to either $+x_c$ or $-x_c$ can lead to rejection; near perpendicularity gives $r=t=0$. The sketch's reject/fail-to-reject markings are qualitative; the exact boundary depends on $n$ and $\alpha$. The slope/projection identities are algebraic, while the exact null t/F laws require the stated error model. The [[wiki/equations/Nested regression F test|one-restriction F/t equivalence]] also applies to a matching coefficient test in multiple regression; its global F test generally tests several restrictions instead.

This is a new angle-based connection, not a re-ingest of the earlier confidence-interval derivation. The signed/unsigned distinction and $n-2$ normalization were checked algebraically and on a negative-correlation numerical example.

## From residual length to adjusted fit

[[wiki/questions/What is the difference between R squared and adjusted R squared|The separate R-squared question]] uses the same residual geometry for a fit comparison. Adding predictor directions enlarges the space onto which the response is projected, so the minimum residual length cannot increase. The [[wiki/equations/R squared and adjusted R squared|canonical score definitions]] compare its squared length with the squared length of the centered response. This explains why ordinary training $R^2$ cannot decrease for nested OLS models fitted to the same observations.

Adjusted $R^2$ additionally divides the residual and total sums of squares by their respective degrees of freedom. Enlarging the fitted space removes residual directions, so the adjusted score rises only when the residual mean square falls. This combines the projection picture with [[wiki/equations/Classical regression t inference#Why there are n minus p residual degrees of freedom|the earlier residual-variance calculation]]. Source: [[wiki/sources/R squared and adjusted R squared conversation - 2026-11-02|A01]]. The explicit geometric bridge is agent synthesis; no new drawing or user derivation was supplied.

## From one null slope to all plausible slopes

Your p. 2 comparison asks whether the observed data are too far from a model constrained to one $b$, then asks which values of $b$ give models close enough to the data. [[wiki/questions/How are regression tests p values and confidence intervals connected|The core question]] preserves that formulation and connects it to p-values and coverage.

In [[wiki/derivations/Slope confidence intervals by inverting an F test|your checked derivation]], the full fit $C$ and candidate-slope fit $D_b$ differ by $(\widehat\beta_1-b)x_c$. You can compute that distance directly or recover it by subtracting the two residual sums of squares. Inverting the resulting F inequality gives the same interval as the t pivot. Both routes express one geometric fact.

The $n=3$ example has only one residual degree of freedom. The interval uses approximately $12.706$ standard errors, the square root of the F critical value $161.448$. The source already includes that square root. The 1.96 illustration on p. 3 is a large-degrees-of-freedom approximation, not the exact multiplier for this drawing.
